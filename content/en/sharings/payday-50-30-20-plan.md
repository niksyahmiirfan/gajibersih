Budgeting apps and spreadsheets work for some people, but many of us just want a rule simple enough to remember on payday. The **50/30/20 split** is one of the easiest.

## How the split works

Take your **take-home pay**, the amount that actually lands in your bank account after EPF, SOCSO, EIS, SKBBK and tax, and divide it three ways:

- **50% for needs**: rent or housing loan, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum loan repayments.
- **30% for wants**: eating out, shopping, streaming, holidays, hobbies.
- **20% for savings and goals**: emergency fund, extra investments, paying down debt faster.

The key detail is to start from take-home pay, not your gross salary. Your EPF contribution is already saving for retirement on your behalf, so the 20% here is *on top of* that.

## A worked example

Say your gross salary is RM5,000 a month. Our [calculator](/take-home-pay-calculator/) estimates your take-home pay at about **RM4,269.95**. Split 50/30/20, that gives roughly:

- Needs: **RM2,134.98**
- Wants: **RM1,280.98**
- Savings: **RM853.99**

You can try your own salary on the [salary pages](/salary/rm-5000-salary-after-epf-and-tax/) to see your take-home figure first.

## Make it automatic

The split works best when you do not have to think about it:

1. **Pay yourself first.** Schedule a transfer of your savings amount to a separate account on payday, before you start spending.
2. **Separate your accounts.** Keep "needs" money in the account your bills come out of, and "wants" money somewhere you can spend freely without guilt.
3. **Build an emergency fund first.** A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. Once that is in place, point your savings slice at longer-term goals.

## When to bend the rule

The numbers are a starting point, not a law. If you live in a city with high rent, needs may take more than half of your pay for a while. That is fine: trim wants first, protect savings where you can, and revisit the split when your salary goes up. A good habit is to save part of every pay rise before your spending catches up.
